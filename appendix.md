# The Katha Upanishad

## A Modern Contemplative Rendering

*Nachiketa’s Conversation with Death*

---

# A Note on This Rendering

This is a modern interpretive rendering of the *Katha Upanishad*, created for contemplative reading. It is not presented as a new scholarly translation from Sanskrit. The rendering follows the traditional two-part, six-valli structure and preserves conventional verse numbering so readers can compare it with other editions. Its wording has been shaped independently with reference to the Sanskrit text and older public-domain English translations, especially F. Max Müller’s nineteenth-century translation.

Certain Sanskrit terms are retained where a single English equivalent would narrow their meaning. Ātman is generally rendered as **Self**, Brahman is retained as **Brahman**, and Om remains **Om**. Yoga is used in its contemplative sense of disciplined stillness and integration. The repeated phrase **“This, indeed, is That”** preserves one of the text’s characteristic refrains: the mystery Nachiketa seeks is not elsewhere, but present in the reality being revealed.

The aim here is clarity without flattening mystery: language that can be read slowly, aloud, and prayerfully, while remaining recognizably rooted in the ancient text.

# Part One

## First Valli — At the House of Death

### 1.1.1

Vajashrava, desiring the rewards of heaven, gave away all that he possessed in sacrifice. He had a son named Nachiketa.

### 1.1.2

As the gifts were being led away, faith awakened in the young Nachiketa. He began to look carefully at what was being offered.

### 1.1.3

The cows were old. They had drunk their last water, eaten their last grass, given their last milk, and could bear no more. Joyless are the worlds reached by one who gives gifts such as these.

### 1.1.4

Nachiketa said to his father, “Father, to whom will you give me?”

He asked a second time, and then a third.

In anger his father answered, “I give you to Death.”

### 1.1.5

Nachiketa thought:

Among many I may be first; among many I may be somewhere in the middle. What purpose will Death have for me today?

### 1.1.6

Look at those who came before us, and look at those who will come after. A human being ripens like grain and falls; like grain, life rises again.

### 1.1.7

A sacred guest enters a house like fire. Such a fire must be met with hospitality.

Bring water, O son of the Sun.

### 1.1.8

When a guest remains in the house of one who does not understand hospitality, unfed and uncared for, the neglect consumes hope and expectation, friendship and good works, children and possessions.

### 1.1.9

When Death returned, he said:

“Honored guest, you have waited in my house for three nights without food. May peace be with you, and may no harm come to me. For each night you waited, choose a boon.”

### 1.1.10

Nachiketa said:

“For my first boon, let my father’s anger pass away. Let his heart become peaceful toward me. When you release me, let him recognize me and welcome me home.”

### 1.1.11

Death replied:

“Your father will know you again and love you as before. When he sees you returned from the mouth of Death, his anger will be gone, and he will sleep peacefully through the night.”

### 1.1.12

Nachiketa said:

“In the heavenly world there is no fear. You are not there as death, and old age brings no terror. Beyond hunger and thirst, beyond sorrow, those who dwell there rejoice.”

### 1.1.13

“You know the sacred fire by which that world is reached. Teach it to me, for I trust your word. Let this be my second boon.”

### 1.1.14

Death said:

“I will teach you. Listen closely. This fire leads toward the heavenly world; it is the support of that world and is hidden deep within.”

### 1.1.15

Death taught him the fire: how the altar was to be formed, how many bricks were needed, and how they were to be placed.

Nachiketa repeated the teaching exactly as he had received it.

### 1.1.16

Pleased, Death said:

“I give you an additional gift. From this day the fire will bear your name: the Nachiketa fire. Receive also this many-colored treasure.”

### 1.1.17

“One who understands this threefold fire, who lives rightly in relation to those who teach and sustain life, and who fulfills the sacred duties, passes beyond the cycle of birth and death.

“Knowing the divine fire from which life arises, one comes to lasting peace.”

### 1.1.18

“One who knows and tends this fire breaks the bonds of death even before dying and enters a joy beyond sorrow.”

### 1.1.19

“This is the fire you chose as your second boon. People will remember it by your name.

“Now, Nachiketa, choose your third boon.”

### 1.1.20

Nachiketa said:

“When a person dies, there is uncertainty. Some say the person continues; others say there is nothing.

“I want to know the truth of this from you. This is my third boon.”

### 1.1.21

Death answered:

“Even the gods have wondered about this. The truth is subtle and difficult to understand.

“Ask me for something else, Nachiketa. Do not press me on this.”

### 1.1.22

Nachiketa replied:

“If even the gods have wondered, then surely the question is profound. And who could teach it better than Death?

“No other gift could equal the answer to this.”

### 1.1.23

Death said:

“Choose children and grandchildren who live a hundred years. Choose cattle, elephants, horses, and gold.

“Choose vast lands, and live as many years as you desire.”

### 1.1.24

“Ask for wealth beyond imagining and a life as long as you wish. Rule a great kingdom.

“I will give you the power to enjoy every desire.”

### 1.1.25

“Whatever pleasures are difficult for mortals to obtain, ask for them. Take beautiful companions, chariots, music, and every delight. Let them serve you.

“Only do not ask me what lies beyond death.”

### 1.1.26

Nachiketa said:

“All these things fade by tomorrow. Pleasure eventually wears out the senses themselves, and even the longest life is brief.

“Keep your chariots, your music, and your pleasures.”

### 1.1.27

“Wealth cannot finally satisfy a human being. If I have met Death himself, what use is wealth to me?

“And however long I live, you will still be there at the end. I choose only the boon I have already asked.”

### 1.1.28

“What mortal, knowing that the body grows old and having glimpsed what does not decay, would be satisfied merely by extending a life devoted to passing pleasures?”

### 1.1.29

“Tell me what lies within this great mystery about which people doubt. Tell me what remains when death comes.

“Nachiketa asks for no other gift.”

# Part One

## Second Valli — The Pleasant and the Good

### 1.2.1

Death said:

The good and the pleasant both approach a human being. The wise examine them and learn to tell them apart.

The wise choose the good; those who seek only comfort choose the pleasant.

### 1.2.2

The good leads one way and the merely pleasant another. Both bind themselves to us in different ways.

Blessed is the one who chooses the good. The one who chooses only what pleases misses the deeper purpose.

### 1.2.3

Nachiketa, you have looked directly at the pleasures that tempt human beings and turned away from them.

You have not been caught in the glittering net where so many lose themselves.

### 1.2.4

Wisdom and ignorance lead in opposite directions.

I see in you, Nachiketa, a true desire for wisdom. Even the promise of many pleasures could not turn you aside.

### 1.2.5

Living in ignorance while imagining themselves wise, people wander in circles, like the blind being led by the blind.

### 1.2.6

The deeper reality does not become clear to one whose mind is absorbed in possessions and appearances.

Thinking, “This world is all there is,” such a person falls again and again under the power of death.

### 1.2.7

Few even hear this teaching clearly. Of those who hear it, few truly understand.

Rare is the teacher who can reveal it, and rare is the student who can receive it.

### 1.2.8

The Self cannot be understood adequately when taught merely as an idea by one who has not realized it. It is subtler than thought.

When revealed by one who knows, the path becomes clear.

### 1.2.9

This understanding is not reached by clever argument alone. It becomes clear when received from one who has seen.

Dear Nachiketa, you are steadfast in your search. May there always be seekers like you.

### 1.2.10

Nachiketa said:

“I know that wealth does not last. What is eternal cannot be reached by what is temporary.

“Yet through the Nachiketa fire I have approached the threshold of what endures.”

### 1.2.11

Death said:

“You have seen the fulfillment of desire, the rewards of action, the vastness of the world, the security people seek, and the power they long to possess.

“Seeing all this, you have remained steady.”

### 1.2.12

The wise one turns inward and discovers the ancient Self, hidden in the depth of the heart, difficult to see and wrapped in mystery.

Knowing that Self, one moves beyond ordinary joy and sorrow.

### 1.2.13

Having heard this teaching and understood it deeply, one distinguishes the Self from all that changes and enters the subtle truth.

Nachiketa, the door is open to you.

### 1.2.14

Nachiketa said:

“Teach me that which is beyond right and wrong, beyond cause and effect, beyond what has been and what is yet to come.”

### 1.2.15

Death said:

“I will tell you the word toward which the Vedas point, for which people undertake discipline, and for which seekers live lives of simplicity.

“That word is Om.”

### 1.2.16

This syllable is Brahman. This syllable points to the Highest.

One who understands it finds support in what is beyond passing things.

### 1.2.17

Om is a sure support, a doorway into the deepest reality.

Knowing its meaning, the seeker comes near to Brahman.

### 1.2.18

The Self is not born, and it does not die.

It did not come from something else, nor will it become something else.

Unborn, ancient, and enduring, it is not destroyed when the body is destroyed.

### 1.2.19

If one thinks, “I kill,” or another thinks, “I am killed,” neither understands.

The Self does not kill, nor can the Self be killed.

### 1.2.20

Smaller than the smallest, greater than the greatest, the Self dwells in the heart of every being.

The one who becomes quiet and free from grasping sees its greatness and is released from sorrow.

### 1.2.21

Sitting still, it travels farther than anything. At rest, it is everywhere.

Who but one who has awakened could know this mystery—the Self that is beyond ordinary pleasure and pain?

### 1.2.22

The wise recognize the bodiless within bodies, the enduring presence within what is impermanent, the vast within the small.

Seeing this, they move beyond grief.

### 1.2.23

The Self is not attained by eloquence, by intellect alone, or by collecting many teachings.

The Self is known by the one who gives oneself wholly to knowing it; then the Self reveals its own nature.

### 1.2.24

But this realization does not come to one who refuses to turn from harmful action, who cannot become still, whose mind is scattered, or whose heart remains restless.

### 1.2.25

Before this reality, the power of rulers and the learning of priests are like a meal laid before the Infinite.

Death itself is only a seasoning.

Who can fully comprehend That?

# Part One

## Third Valli — The Chariot and the Inner Self

### 1.3.1

Two dwell in the secret chamber of the heart and taste the results of their actions.

Those who know Brahman speak of them as light and shadow. So do those who tend the sacred fire and those who have crossed to the farther shore.

### 1.3.2

May we understand the Nachiketa fire, the bridge for those who live by sacred action, and may we also know the imperishable Brahman, the farther shore for those who seek freedom from fear.

### 1.3.3

Know the Self as the master of a chariot.

Know the body as the chariot itself.

The intellect is the charioteer, and the mind is the reins.

### 1.3.4

The senses are the horses, and the objects of the senses are the roads they travel.

The wise call the Self, joined with body, senses, and mind, the one who experiences the journey.

### 1.3.5

When understanding is weak and the mind is undisciplined, the senses run wild like untrained horses beneath an unskilled driver.

### 1.3.6

But when understanding is clear and the mind is steady, the senses become obedient like well-trained horses beneath a skillful driver.

### 1.3.7

One who lacks discernment, whose mind is uncontrolled and whose life is impure, does not reach the goal but continues in the cycle of becoming.

### 1.3.8

One who has discernment, whose mind is steady and whose life is purified, reaches the goal from which there is no return.

### 1.3.9

With wisdom as charioteer and a disciplined mind as reins, the traveler reaches the end of the road—the highest dwelling of the all-pervading Reality.

### 1.3.10

Beyond the senses are the objects they perceive.

Beyond those objects is the mind.

Beyond the mind is the discerning intellect.

Beyond intellect is the great Self.

### 1.3.11

Beyond the great Self is the unmanifest.

Beyond the unmanifest is the Person, the indwelling Spirit.

Beyond that there is nothing.

This is the end of the journey; this is the highest goal.

### 1.3.12

This Self is hidden in every being and does not readily appear.

It is seen by those whose vision has become subtle and clear through deep attention.

### 1.3.13

Let the wise gather speech into the mind, the mind into discerning awareness, awareness into the great Self, and the great Self into the peaceful Self.

### 1.3.14

Rise. Awake. Seek those who know, and learn.

The path is sharp like the edge of a razor, difficult to walk and difficult to cross—so the wise have said.

### 1.3.15

That which is beyond sound, touch, form, taste, and smell; which does not decay; which has no beginning and no end; which is beyond even the great principle of becoming—

knowing That, one is freed from the mouth of death.

### 1.3.16

The wise person who hears and shares this ancient story of Nachiketa, spoken by Death himself, is honored in the realm of Brahman.

### 1.3.17

And one who recites this deepest teaching with reverence among seekers, or at a sacred remembrance of those who have died, prepares the heart for what is immeasurable.

# Part Two

## First Valli — Turning Inward

### 2.1.1

The Self-existent One made the senses turn outward; therefore we usually look outside ourselves and do not see the Self within.

But a wise person, longing for what does not die, turns the gaze inward and discovers the inner Self.

### 2.1.2

Those who are immature chase outward pleasures and become caught in the wide net of death.

The wise, knowing what endures, do not seek permanence among things that cannot last.

### 2.1.3

That by which we know form, taste, smell, sound, touch, and desire—by what could that Knower itself be known?

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.4

That by which we experience both waking and dreaming is the great, all-pervading Self.

Knowing this, the wise no longer grieve.

### 2.1.5

One who knows the Self as the living presence that receives experience and as the Lord of what has been and what will be, no longer hides from reality.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.6

The One who was present before creation, who arose through the power of life and entered the heart of beings—

seeing that One within the elements, one sees That.

### 2.1.7

Aditi, the boundless mother of the gods, arises with life and enters the heart.

She is born with beings and dwells among the elements.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.8

Like fire hidden within two pieces of wood, carefully guarded like an unborn child, the sacred presence is hidden within.

It is awakened by those who tend the inner fire with attention.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.9

From That the sun rises, and into That the sun sets.

All the powers of life rest in That; nothing passes beyond it.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.10

What is here is also there; what is there is also here.

One who sees only separation moves from death to death.

### 2.1.11

This is known through the purified mind: there is no ultimate division here.

One who sees reality as divided moves again and again through death.

### 2.1.12

The Person, no larger than a thumb, dwells in the center of the heart and is Lord of what has been and what will be.

Knowing this, one no longer shrinks away in fear.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.13

The inner Person, luminous like a flame without smoke, is the same today and tomorrow, the same beyond time.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.1.14

As rain falling on a mountain runs down in many separate streams, so one who sees only separate things runs after them in every direction.

### 2.1.15

As pure water poured into pure water becomes one with it, so the Self of the awakened one rests in the One.

# Part Two

## Second Valli — The City of Eleven Gates

### 2.2.1

There is a city with eleven gates belonging to the unborn, undivided Consciousness.

One who meditates upon the indwelling One becomes free from sorrow and, becoming free, is released.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.2.2

The One is the swan in the clear sky, the presence in the open air, the fire at the altar, the guest in the house.

The One dwells in human beings, in the gods, in truth, in the vast heavens, in water, in earth, in sacrifice, and in the mountains.

The One is the great Reality.

### 2.2.3

The One sends the life-breath upward and draws the downward breath inward.

At the center sits the radiant presence, honored by all the powers of life.

### 2.2.4

When the indwelling Self is released from the body, what remains of the body then?

By what does it live?

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.2.5

No mortal lives merely by the inward breath or the outward breath.

Life depends upon something deeper, upon That in which both breaths have their ground.

### 2.2.6

Nachiketa, I will tell you again the secret of the eternal Brahman and what becomes of the Self after death.

### 2.2.7

Some enter another womb and take embodied form again, according to their actions and understanding.

Others enter other states of existence.

### 2.2.8

The One who remains awake while others sleep, shaping the forms of desire within the dream—

That is the pure.

That is Brahman.

That alone is called immortal.

All worlds rest in That; nothing passes beyond it.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.2.9

As one fire enters the world and takes the shape of whatever it burns, so the one Self within all beings appears to take their many forms, while also remaining beyond them.

### 2.2.10

As one air enters the world and seems to take the shape of what contains it, so the one Self within all beings appears in many forms, while remaining beyond every form.

### 2.2.11

The sun is the eye of the world, yet it is not stained by the defects of the eyes that behold it.

In the same way, the one Self within all beings is not stained by the suffering of the world, for it is also beyond the world.

### 2.2.12

The One, the inner Self of all beings, makes the one reality appear as many.

Those who see this presence within themselves discover lasting joy—not those who remain unaware.

### 2.2.13

The Eternal among the changing, consciousness within the conscious, the One who fulfills the deepest longing of the many—

those who see That within themselves find lasting peace.

### 2.2.14

The sages know a joy beyond ordinary pleasure and say, “This is That.”

How can it be known?

Does it shine by its own light, or does it shine through another?

### 2.2.15

The sun does not illumine That, nor the moon, nor the stars.

Lightning does not illumine it, nor earthly fire.

By its light all these shine.

Because That shines, everything is revealed.

# Part Two

## Third Valli — The Tree of Life and the Freedom Beyond Death

### 2.3.1

There is an ancient tree with its roots above and its branches below.

Its root is the pure, the Brahman, the immortal.

All worlds rest in it, and none passes beyond it.

This, indeed, is That.

### 2.3.2

The whole universe, whatever exists, arises from and moves within the life of Brahman.

It is a great mystery, like a raised thunderbolt.

Those who truly know it become free from fear.

### 2.3.3

Through awe before That, fire burns; through awe, the sun shines.

Through awe, the powers of wind and storm move, and Death itself performs its work.

### 2.3.4

If a person realizes this truth before the body falls away, freedom is found.

If not, one returns again to the worlds of becoming.

### 2.3.5

Here, in the awakened mind, the Self may be seen as clearly as a face in a mirror.

In the world of the ancestors it is seen as in a dream; in subtler realms, as a reflection in trembling water; in the highest realm, as light and shadow made distinct.

### 2.3.6

Knowing that the senses have their own nature, knowing their rising and their setting, and knowing that the Self is distinct from them, the wise no longer grieve.

### 2.3.7

Beyond the senses is the mind.

Beyond the mind is the discerning intellect.

Beyond intellect is the great Self.

Beyond the great Self is the unmanifest.

### 2.3.8

Beyond the unmanifest is the Person, all-pervading and without visible mark.

Knowing That, a person is released and enters immortality.

### 2.3.9

Its form cannot be seen with the eyes.

No one sees it as an object.

It is revealed in the heart through a purified understanding.

Those who know this become immortal.

### 2.3.10

When the five senses become still, together with the mind, and the intellect no longer rushes outward, this is called the highest state.

### 2.3.11

This steady resting of the senses is called yoga.

In that stillness one must remain awake and attentive, for yoga can be gained and it can be lost.

### 2.3.12

The Self cannot be grasped by speech, by sight, or by the ordinary mind.

How can it be known except by the one who realizes, “It is”?

### 2.3.13

It must first be approached as truly existing; then its deeper nature becomes clear.

To the one who trusts the reality of the Self and seeks it, the Self reveals itself.

### 2.3.14

When all the desires that cling to the heart fall away, the mortal becomes immortal and discovers Brahman here and now.

### 2.3.15

When all the knots of the heart are loosened, the mortal becomes immortal.

This is the heart of the teaching.

### 2.3.16

A hundred and one subtle channels rise from the heart.

One of them ascends toward the crown of the head. Moving upward through it, the seeker reaches immortality; the others lead toward different paths of becoming.

### 2.3.17

The inner Person, no larger than a thumb, dwells always in the heart of every being.

With steady discernment, draw the Self from the body as one draws the tender inner stalk from a blade of grass.

Know this Self as pure and immortal—

yes, pure and immortal.

### 2.3.18

Nachiketa received this wisdom from Death, together with the full discipline of inner realization.

Knowing Brahman, he became free from impurity and free from death.

So it is for anyone who comes to know the indwelling Self.
