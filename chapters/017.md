# Day 17 — Training the Horses

> **1.3.5–9**
>
> When understanding is weak and the mind is undisciplined, the senses run wild like untrained horses beneath an unskilled driver.
>
> But when understanding is clear and the mind is steady, the senses become obedient like well-trained horses beneath a skillful driver.
>
> One who lacks discernment, whose mind is uncontrolled and whose life is impure, does not reach the goal but continues in the cycle of becoming.
>
> One who has discernment, whose mind is steady and whose life is purified, reaches the goal from which there is no return.
>
> With wisdom as charioteer and a disciplined mind as reins, the traveler reaches the end of the road—the highest dwelling of the all-pervading Reality.

Yesterday Yama gave Nachiketa the image of the chariot. Today he sets it in motion. The question is no longer simply what the horses, reins, and charioteer represent, but what happens when they must travel together down an actual road.

Untrained horses follow whatever attracts them. One pulls toward food, another toward pleasure, another toward safety, another toward whatever suddenly appears interesting. If the reins are loose and the charioteer cannot guide them, the problem is not that the horses are evil; they are simply doing what horses naturally do.

Our senses and desires behave much the same way. Something catches the eye, a craving appears, anger rises, or an opportunity for pleasure presents itself, and movement begins almost immediately. Sometimes we notice the decision only after we have already made it.

Yama calls the alternative discipline, but discipline here need not mean hostility toward ourselves. A skilled charioteer does not spend the journey screaming at the horses or punishing them for wanting to run. Training creates responsiveness, so that the energy of the horses can serve the direction of the journey rather than continually changing it.

This requires a steady mind. Between impulse and action, even a small space can appear in which we recognize what is happening. *There is wanting. There is irritation. There is fear. There is the urge to reach for something.* Naming the movement can loosen its claim upon us.

The desire does not necessarily disappear. The horses may still pull strongly against the reins, and sometimes they may pull harder precisely because we have stopped automatically following them. But an urge can be present without becoming an instruction.

This is one of the quiet freedoms cultivated through contemplative practice. We learn that not everything arising within us must be obeyed, suppressed, or solved. Some movements can simply be felt until they change on their own.

Over time, something begins to shift. The horses learn the reins, the hands holding the reins become steadier, and the charioteer becomes more familiar with the road. What once required exhausting effort may gradually become a different way of traveling.

There will still be moments when the chariot veers off course. Yama's image need not become another demand for perfection. When we realize we have wandered, awareness itself gives us the possibility of returning.

And returning may be more important than never wandering.

The destination Yama describes is not merely better self-control. The whole discipline serves something deeper: the journey toward the all-pervading Reality. We learn to guide desire not because desire is shameful, but because we do not want every passing attraction to determine the direction of our lives.

The horses have tremendous energy. Properly guided, that energy can carry us home.

**Mantra**

> **I can feel desire without allowing desire to choose my direction.**
