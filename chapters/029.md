# Day 29 — The Knots of the Heart

> **2.3.14–17**
>
> When all the desires that cling to the heart fall away, the mortal becomes immortal and discovers Brahman here and now.
>
> When all the knots of the heart are loosened, the mortal becomes immortal.
>
> This is the heart of the teaching.
>
> A hundred and one subtle channels rise from the heart.
>
> One of them ascends toward the crown of the head.
>
> Moving upward through it, the seeker reaches immortality; the others lead toward different paths of becoming.
>
> The inner Person, no larger than a thumb, dwells always in the heart of every being.
>
> With steady discernment, draw the Self from the body as one draws the tender inner stalk from a blade of grass.
>
> Know this Self as pure and immortal—yes, pure and immortal.

Yama now speaks of freedom in intimate language: the desires that cling to the heart fall away, and the knots of the heart are loosened. The image is gentle. He does not say that the heart must be broken open or that desire must be violently destroyed.

A knot forms when something is pulled tight around itself. Fear can become a knot, as can resentment, craving, shame, certainty, or the desperate need for life to unfold in one particular way. Over time, these inner tensions can feel so familiar that we no longer recognize how tightly we are holding them.

Yama connects liberation with loosening. This is different from getting everything we want, and it is also different from learning never to want anything. The deeper freedom is that desire no longer has to contract the whole heart around itself.

We may still want comfort, health, love, security, recognition, or relief from pain. Those desires can be present without becoming the condition upon which our peace depends. Something within us can remain open even when life does not immediately satisfy what we want.

This is why the teaching reaches beyond self-denial. If we merely suppress desire, the knot may tighten in another form. We can become attached to being detached, proud of our discipline, or frightened by the return of an impulse we thought we had conquered.

Loosening is subtler. We notice what the heart is gripping and allow the grip to soften. We breathe around the wanting rather than immediately obeying it or fighting it.

Sometimes the knot is created by the words *must* and *cannot*. This must happen for me to be okay. This cannot happen because I would not survive it. I must become this kind of person. I cannot bear to be seen in that way.

The contemplative path gives these statements more room. We begin to discover that an uncomfortable feeling can be present without becoming an emergency. A longing can remain unanswered for a while without requiring us to fill the space immediately.

Yama says that when the knots loosen, immortality is discovered “here and now.” Again, he refuses to make freedom merely a reward after death. Nachiketa came seeking what happens at the end of life, and Death keeps pointing him toward a transformation available within life itself.

The final image is delicate: drawing the inner stalk from a blade of grass. The Self is not ripped away through force but discerned carefully from everything with which we have confused it. Body, personality, desire, memory, and fear remain part of our experience, but they no longer have to define the deepest truth of what we are.

This may be the heart of Yama’s teaching. Freedom comes as we stop fastening our whole identity to what changes. The heart gradually opens its clenched hand.

Tomorrow, Nachiketa’s journey will end.

But today he is given something perhaps more important than an explanation of death: a way of living in which fear and desire no longer have to hold the heart so tightly.

**Mantra**

> **I let the knots of the heart loosen.**
