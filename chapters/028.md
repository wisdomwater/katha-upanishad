# Day 28 — It Is

> **2.3.12–13**
>
> The Self cannot be grasped by speech, by sight, or by the ordinary mind.
>
> How can it be known except by the one who realizes, “It is”?
>
> It must first be approached as truly existing; then its deeper nature becomes clear.
>
> To the one who trusts the reality of the Self and seeks it, the Self reveals itself.

Yama has taken Nachiketa almost as far as language can go. Again and again he has pointed toward the Self through images: fire hidden in wood, light behind light, the rider in the chariot, the presence in the city of eleven gates. Now he tells him that the Self cannot finally be grasped by speech, sight, or the ordinary mind.

This is a strange kind of teaching. Usually we think that knowing means being able to define something, describe it accurately, or hold it clearly before the mind. Yama suggests that the deepest reality does not become available in that way.

Instead, he offers a remarkably simple phrase: **It is.**

There is a humility in those words. They do not tell us what the Self looks like, where it is located, or how it fits into a system of ideas. They simply affirm that reality is deeper than what our senses and concepts are able to contain.

This is not the same as believing anything without question. Nachiketa has questioned everything from the beginning, and Yama has encouraged discernment rather than blind acceptance. But there comes a point where analysis reaches its limit and a different posture becomes possible.

We already live this way in many areas of life. We cannot fully explain consciousness, love, beauty, or the fact that anything exists at all, yet we do not therefore conclude that these things are unreal. Sometimes reality arrives before our explanations do.

Perhaps contemplation trains us to become comfortable at that threshold. We allow ourselves to experience what is present without immediately demanding that it fit into a concept. We sit with the simple fact of being alive, aware, breathing, and present.

The mind may ask, *What is this?* That question can be useful, but for a moment we can also allow another response: *It is.*

A sensation arises. It is.

A thought appears. It is.

Awareness is present. It is.

The world exists before we explain it, and perhaps the deepest mysteries of the Self are discovered in the same way. Not through passivity, but through an attention willing to receive before it defines.

Yama says that when the Self is approached as truly real, its deeper nature becomes clear. This sounds less like solving a puzzle and more like learning to recognize something through relationship. We come to know by remaining near.

There is also a quiet challenge here to our need for certainty. Sometimes we refuse to approach what cannot first be proven to our satisfaction. Yet some forms of knowing only become possible after we enter the experience itself.

The contemplative life does not ask us to stop thinking. It asks us to recognize when thinking has reached the edge of its usefulness. Beyond that edge, we may discover that mystery is not emptiness.

Something is here.

Something has always been here.

Before we name it, explain it, defend it, or doubt it, Yama invites us simply to become still enough to say:

It is.

**Mantra**

> **It is. I rest before the mystery I cannot contain.**
