# Day 5 — What Remains When We Die?

> **1.1.20–22**
>
> Nachiketa said:
>
> “When a person dies, there is uncertainty. Some say the person continues; others say there is nothing.
>
> “I want to know the truth of this from you. This is my third boon.”
>
> Death answered:
>
> “Even the gods have wondered about this. The truth is subtle and difficult to understand.
>
> “Ask me for something else, Nachiketa. Do not press me on this.”
>
> Nachiketa replied:
>
> “If even the gods have wondered, then surely the question is profound. And who could teach it better than Death?
>
> “No other gift could equal the answer to this.”

At last Nachiketa asks the question that has been waiting beneath everything else. His father's angry words sent him toward Death, but now that he stands before Death himself, the question becomes universal. What happens to us when we die? Does something remain, or does the person simply cease to be?

There is a striking humility in the way the *Katha Upanishad* introduces the question. Nachiketa does not pretend to know the answer. More surprisingly, Death does not immediately claim that the answer is obvious. “Even the gods have wondered about this,” he says. The mystery of death is allowed to remain a mystery before it becomes a teaching.

We often find uncertainty difficult to bear, especially here. Death touches nearly every attachment we have: our bodies, the people we love, the identities we have built, the experiences we still hope to have. We want assurance that none of it will finally be lost. It is understandable that human beings have filled the unknown with images of heavens and rebirths, resurrections and dissolutions, ancestors and eternal souls.

Nachiketa asks for something deeper than reassurance. He wants to know what is true.

Death warns him that the truth is subtle. This may be one of the most important clues for everything that follows. Yama will not simply describe a place where Nachiketa goes after his body dies. Instead, he will gradually challenge Nachiketa's understanding of the one who is asking the question. Before we can ask what happens to the self after death, we may need to ask what this **self** actually is.

That movement changes the question. We usually approach death from the outside, imagining a future event in which the life we know suddenly stops. Yama will invite Nachiketa to approach it from within, looking beneath body, sensation, thought, desire, and personality toward the mysterious ground of awareness itself. The question will slowly shift from *What happens to me when I die?* toward *What is this “me” that I am so afraid of losing?*

Nachiketa does not know any of this yet. He only knows that no other gift could equal the answer. He could have asked Death for something safer, something easier to understand, something immediately useful. Instead, he chooses to remain with the question that reaches beneath all the others.

Perhaps we can do the same without demanding certainty. We can acknowledge our hopes about death and our fears about death while allowing ourselves not to know what we do not know. The mystery does not need to be solved before it can become a doorway into deeper attention.

For now, Nachiketa simply stands before Death and asks.

And Death, before answering, makes sure that he truly wants to know.

> **I can meet the mystery without pretending to know.**
