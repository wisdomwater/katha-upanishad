# Day 9 — The Glittering Net

> **1.2.3–6**
>
> Nachiketa, you have looked directly at the pleasures that tempt human beings and turned away from them.
>
> You have not been caught in the glittering net where so many lose themselves.
>
> Wisdom and ignorance lead in opposite directions.
>
> I see in you, Nachiketa, a true desire for wisdom. Even the promise of many pleasures could not turn you aside.
>
> Living in ignorance while imagining themselves wise, people wander in circles, like the blind being led by the blind.
>
> The deeper reality does not become clear to one whose mind is absorbed in possessions and appearances.
>
> Thinking, “This world is all there is,” such a person falls again and again under the power of death.

Yama calls the world of temptation a glittering net. The image is worth lingering over. A net captures because it is difficult to see, but this one also glitters. What entangles us is often attractive.

Nachiketa has just been offered wealth, longevity, pleasure, status, music, and beauty. None of these needed to appear threatening. They could have filled an entire life with activity and enjoyment while quietly ensuring that he never returned to his original question. The net did not need to hurt him. It only needed to keep him occupied.

Our nets can be just as bright. There is always something asking for our attention: possessions to acquire, experiences to arrange, accomplishments to pursue, opinions to defend, images to curate, information to consume. Even good things can accumulate until our lives become so crowded with surfaces that we rarely wonder what lies beneath them.

The danger Yama describes is not merely ignorance. It is **ignorance that believes itself to be wisdom**. We can become so accustomed to our way of seeing that we forget it is a way of seeing. We mistake our assumptions for reality itself. Like travelers following one another through darkness, we may move confidently without ever asking whether anyone actually knows the way.

This is why the contemplative life involves a certain humility. It creates space around what we think we know. We begin to notice how much of our understanding has been inherited, assumed, repeated, or assembled from appearances. We learn to hold even our cherished explanations with open hands.

Yama says that the deeper reality remains hidden when the mind is absorbed in possessions and appearances. This need not mean that the material world is false or unimportant. The Upanishad will eventually find the sacred presence within everything. The problem is not that we see the world; it is that we see only its surface.

A person can possess many things and still feel that something is missing. We can know an enormous amount and still not know ourselves. We can fill every quiet moment and then wonder why the deeper questions seem so far away.

Perhaps the glittering net is simply whatever keeps us moving without ever allowing us to ask where we are going.

Nachiketa does something different. He pauses. He looks at what Yama offers. He recognizes its beauty and its impermanence, and he remembers why he came.

That remembering is a kind of freedom.

We do not have to tear down the world or reject its pleasures. We may only need to notice when something beautiful has become a net, when something useful has become an identity, or when something temporary has begun asking us to treat it as ultimate.

The net loosens the moment we can see it.

> **I remember what matters beneath what glitters.**
