# Day 20 — Turn Your Gaze Inward

> **2.1.1–5**
>
> The Self-existent One made the senses turn outward; therefore we usually look outside ourselves and do not see the Self within.
>
> But a wise person, longing for what does not die, turns the gaze inward and discovers the inner Self.
>
> Those who are immature chase outward pleasures and become caught in the wide net of death.
>
> The wise, knowing what endures, do not seek permanence among things that cannot last.
>
> That by which we know form, taste, smell, sound, touch, and desire—by what could that Knower itself be known?
>
> This, indeed, is That.
>
> That by which we experience both waking and dreaming is the great, all-pervading Self.
>
> Knowing this, the wise no longer grieve.
>
> One who knows the Self as the living presence that receives experience and as the Lord of what has been and what will be, no longer hides from reality.
>
> This, indeed, is That.

Yama now names one of the most basic movements of human attention: the senses turn outward. Our eyes look toward forms, our ears toward sounds, our hands toward what can be touched, and our minds toward whatever appears before them. From the moment we wake, attention flows naturally into the world.

There is nothing wrong with this movement. The senses allow us to encounter the astonishing richness of life: faces, music, food, sunlight, conversation, work, beauty, and the physical presence of those we love. The Upanishad does not ask us to close ourselves off from the world, but it does suggest that outward attention gives us only part of the picture.

We become so accustomed to noticing what we experience that we rarely notice the one who is experiencing. We see a tree but do not wonder about the awareness in which seeing occurs. We hear music but seldom turn toward the mysterious capacity by which sound becomes experience.

Yama therefore asks a strange question: by what could the Knower itself be known? Anything we normally know becomes an object before awareness—a thought, sensation, memory, body, or emotion. But when we try to make awareness itself into an object, it seems to recede from us.

This is where turning inward becomes more than introspection. We are not simply replacing attention to the outer world with attention to our private thoughts and feelings. Thoughts and feelings are also things we experience, and the Upanishad is inviting us deeper still, toward that by which experience itself is known.

The movement is subtle. We notice a sound, then notice our awareness of the sound. We notice a thought, then recognize that the thought is appearing within awareness. We notice a desire pulling us toward something and discover that even the desire can be observed.

Again and again, Yama asks us to become curious about the Knower.

This does not require rejecting outward life. In fact, turning inward may eventually allow us to return to the world with greater freedom. When we no longer expect every object, pleasure, achievement, or relationship to provide the permanence we seek, we may become more capable of receiving them as they actually are.

The temporary can be loved without being asked to become eternal.

Then Yama introduces a refrain that will accompany the next part of the journey: **This, indeed, is That.** Nachiketa has traveled to the house of Death looking for some distant answer to the mystery of existence. Yama keeps pointing him back toward the reality already present in every act of knowing.

The seeker looks outward for the thing that will finally answer the longing. The contemplative path gently reverses the gaze. Before reaching for something else, we become still enough to notice the awareness in which reaching itself occurs.

We do not have to remain turned inward forever. The eyes will open, the senses will move outward, and the world will call for our attention again. But perhaps we return differently after discovering that the mystery we sought in the distance was also quietly looking through our own eyes.

**Mantra**

> **I turn inward and notice the Knower within.**
