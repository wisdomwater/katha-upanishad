# Day 27 — When the Senses Become Still

> **2.3.5–11**
>
> Here, in the awakened mind, the Self may be seen as clearly as a face in a mirror.
>
> In the world of the ancestors it is seen as in a dream; in subtler realms, as a reflection in trembling water; in the highest realm, as light and shadow made distinct.
>
> Knowing that the senses have their own nature, knowing their rising and their setting, and knowing that the Self is distinct from them, the wise no longer grieve.
>
> Beyond the senses is the mind.
>
> Beyond the mind is the discerning intellect.
>
> Beyond intellect is the great Self.
>
> Beyond the great Self is the unmanifest.
>
> Beyond the unmanifest is the Person, all-pervading and without visible mark.
>
> Knowing That, a person is released and enters immortality.
>
> Its form cannot be seen with the eyes.
>
> No one sees it as an object.
>
> It is revealed in the heart through a purified understanding.
>
> Those who know this become immortal.
>
> When the five senses become still, together with the mind, and the intellect no longer rushes outward, this is called the highest state.
>
> This steady resting of the senses is called yoga.
>
> In that stillness one must remain awake and attentive, for yoga can be gained and it can be lost.

Yama now speaks as directly as he has anywhere in the text about contemplative practice. The senses become still, the mind settles, and even the intellect stops rushing outward in search of something to grasp. This steady resting, he says, is yoga.

Stillness here is not unconsciousness. Yama immediately adds that one must remain awake and attentive. The goal is not to dull the senses or disappear from experience, but to become so present that the usual momentum of grasping begins to quiet.

We know how quickly attention can be scattered. A sound pulls us one way, a thought another, a memory opens, a desire appears, and soon we are far from where we began. The senses are not doing anything wrong; they are simply responding to the world as they were made to do.

Yoga, in this passage, is the gathering of that scattered energy. The eyes can see without chasing every form, the ears can hear without following every sound, and thoughts can arise without immediately becoming journeys we must take. The whole inner system begins to rest.

This does not require forcing ourselves into perfect silence. In fact, force usually creates more agitation. Stillness is learned through repeated returning, the gentle act of noticing that attention has wandered and allowing it to come home again.

Yama compares clear realization to seeing a face in a mirror. A mirror reveals well when its surface is steady and clean, while trembling water distorts whatever it reflects. The problem is not that reality is absent, but that a restless surface makes it difficult to see clearly.

The image turns practice from acquisition into clarification. We do not need to manufacture the Self or produce some rare spiritual object. We become quieter so that what is already present can be seen without so much distortion.

There is a patience required here. Yama says yoga can be gained and it can be lost. One period of stillness does not permanently remove distraction, and yesterday's clarity does not guarantee today's.

That need not discourage us. It simply means the practice is alive. We return because the mind wanders, just as we breathe again because one breath was not enough.

Over time, we may begin to notice that stillness is not the absence of life. It can be a deeper intimacy with life, free from the compulsion to pursue every sensation and solve every thought. The senses remain open, but they no longer have to drag the whole chariot behind them.

Nachiketa came seeking what does not die. Yama keeps bringing him back to a very immediate practice: become still enough to see what is already here. Not asleep, not numb, not detached from the world, but awake.

This is yoga.

Not escape from experience, but steadiness within it.

**Mantra**

> **I become still without becoming asleep.**
