# Day 1 — The Gift That Costs Nothing

> **1.1.1–3**
>
> Vajashrava, desiring the rewards of heaven, gave away all that he possessed in sacrifice. He had a son named Nachiketa.
>
> As the gifts were being led away, faith awakened in the young Nachiketa. He began to look carefully at what was being offered.
>
> The cows were old. They had drunk their last water, eaten their last grass, given their last milk, and could bear no more. Joyless are the worlds reached by one who gives gifts such as these.

The *Katha Upanishad* begins with a religious act. Vajashrava is making a sacrifice, giving away his possessions in the hope of receiving the blessings promised to those who give generously. From the outside, everything appears admirable. The ritual is being performed, the gifts are being offered, and the proper religious gestures are being made.

But Nachiketa notices the cows.

They are old and exhausted. They have already given everything they have to give. They can no longer provide milk or produce offspring, and whoever receives them will inherit little more than the burden of caring for them. Vajashrava is technically giving something away, but he is giving away what no longer costs him very much.

The text says that as Nachiketa watches, **faith awakens in him**. That is an interesting description, because we might expect faith to mean accepting what his father is doing without question. Instead, faith makes Nachiketa look more closely. Something within him recognizes the distance between the appearance of generosity and generosity itself.

Perhaps this is where spiritual awakening often begins. Not with certainty, but with the uncomfortable recognition that something does not quite fit. We notice the gap between what we say and what we do, between the values we profess and the choices we actually make. The temptation is to look away from that gap, particularly when the outward form appears respectable.

There are many ways to give away old cows. We can offer someone our attention while keeping part of ourselves occupied elsewhere. We can apologize without surrendering the need to be right. We can practice generosity with what we no longer need while guarding what we most fear losing. Even spiritual practice can become a transaction in which we offer just enough effort, discipline, or devotion to obtain the peace or blessing we hope to receive.

Nachiketa does not yet know where his questioning will lead. He has not met Death. He has not heard about the Self, learned the meaning of Om, or discovered what Yama will eventually teach him about immortality. At this moment, he is simply a young person watching cows being led away and allowing himself to see what is actually happening.

That may be enough for a beginning.

Contemplation does not always begin by closing our eyes. Sometimes it begins by opening them. Before we can discover what is deepest and most enduring, we may first have to notice the places where our lives have become automatic, where outward appearances have replaced inward truth, and where we are offering something that costs us almost nothing.

Nachiketa's journey toward the deathless begins with this small act of honesty.

Perhaps ours can too.

> **Let me see clearly what I am giving—and what I am holding back.**
