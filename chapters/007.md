# Day 7 — Keep Your Chariots

> **1.1.26–29**
>
> Nachiketa said:
>
> “All these things fade by tomorrow. Pleasure eventually wears out the senses themselves, and even the longest life is brief.
>
> “Keep your chariots, your music, and your pleasures.”
>
> “Wealth cannot finally satisfy a human being. If I have met Death himself, what use is wealth to me?
>
> “And however long I live, you will still be there at the end. I choose only the boon I have already asked.”
>
> “What mortal, knowing that the body grows old and having glimpsed what does not decay, would be satisfied merely by extending a life devoted to passing pleasures?”
>
> “Tell me what lies within this great mystery about which people doubt. Tell me what remains when death comes.
>
> “Nachiketa asks for no other gift.”

Nachiketa looks at everything Death has offered him and notices one thing they all have in common: they end.

The chariots will decay. The music will stop. Pleasure will fade. Wealth may postpone certain fears, but it cannot remove the final one. Even the extraordinarily long life Yama offers is still measured in years, and however large that number becomes, Death waits at the other end.

So Nachiketa says something wonderfully simple: **Keep your chariots.**

He is not declaring pleasure evil or insisting that a spiritual person should reject everything enjoyable. His insight is subtler than that. He has recognized that temporary things cannot provide permanent satisfaction. The problem comes when we ask them to do so.

This is easy to understand and difficult to live. We have all experienced the strange cycle of wanting something, receiving it, enjoying it, and eventually discovering ourselves wanting again. Sometimes the satisfaction lasts months or years. Sometimes it lasts only minutes. Yet the mind keeps whispering that perhaps the next thing will be different.

There is nothing particularly wrong with this movement. Hunger leads us toward food. Curiosity leads us toward discovery. Desire brings people together and gives energy to creativity, work, relationship, and play. The trouble begins when desire carries a promise it cannot keep: *This will finally be enough.*

Nachiketa sees through that promise.

Perhaps this is one meaning of spiritual maturity—not the disappearance of desire, but a changing relationship with it. We can enjoy what comes without requiring it to complete us. We can receive pleasure without demanding permanence from it. We can even feel the ache of wanting something we do not have without assuming that the ache must immediately be removed.

That creates a small but profound freedom. Wanting can be present without becoming a command.

Nachiketa is able to say no to Yama because something matters to him more than immediate satisfaction. He wants to know what remains when everything else has been taken away. His question has become stronger than his appetite for distraction.

Most of us will not stand before Death while celestial chariots and unimaginable riches are offered to us. Our choices are usually much smaller. We encounter them in the quiet moment when we notice ourselves reaching automatically for the next thing—the next purchase, the next bite, the next achievement, the next distraction, the next reassurance.

Perhaps we can pause there.

Not because the thing is forbidden, but because for a moment we can ask what we are really seeking from it.

Nachiketa has discovered that the answer to his deepest longing cannot be another object of desire. So he lets the chariots remain with Death and continues toward the mystery.

**Mantra**

> **I can enjoy what passes without asking it to satisfy what is eternal.**
