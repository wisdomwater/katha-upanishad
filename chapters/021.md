# Day 21 — This Is That

> **2.1.6–9**
>
> The One who was present before creation, who arose through the power of life and entered the heart of beings—seeing that One within the elements, one sees That.
>
> Aditi, the boundless mother of the gods, arises with life and enters the heart.
>
> She is born with beings and dwells among the elements.
>
> This, indeed, is That.
>
> Like fire hidden within two pieces of wood, carefully guarded like an unborn child, the sacred presence is hidden within.
>
> It is awakened by those who tend the inner fire with attention.
>
> This, indeed, is That.
>
> From That the sun rises, and into That the sun sets.
>
> All the powers of life rest in That; nothing passes beyond it.
>
> This, indeed, is That.

Yama now begins repeating a phrase that gathers the whole teaching into a few words: **This, indeed, is That.** The mystery Nachiketa has been seeking is not confined to another realm or hidden somewhere beyond the world. It is present within life itself, within the elements, within the heart, within the rising and setting of the sun.

The language is expansive and intimate at the same time. The One was present before creation, yet also enters the heart of living beings. The same reality that gives rise to the cosmos is described as hidden within us, like fire concealed inside wood.

That image is especially beautiful. Fire may be present in the wood without being visible, waiting for the right conditions to reveal itself. In the same way, Yama suggests that the sacred is not absent simply because we do not perceive it.

The problem may be less about finding what is missing and more about awakening to what is already here. We spend so much of life searching for something more: a different circumstance, a clearer answer, a deeper experience, a moment in which everything finally feels complete. The refrain interrupts that movement and turns our attention back toward the present.

**This is That.**

This breath. This body. This moment. This ordinary world of sunlight, wood, water, fatigue, beauty, work, grief, and relationship may be the very place where the deeper reality is encountered.

That does not mean every experience is pleasant or that suffering should be romanticized. It means the sacred is not dependent upon ideal conditions. If Brahman is the ground of all that is, then there is no ordinary place from which it is absent.

The image of tending the inner fire gives us a clue about practice. We do not create the fire from nothing; we attend to what is already latent. Through stillness, attention, ethical living, and repeated return, what was hidden may become perceptible.

There is humility in this kind of spirituality. We do not conquer the mystery or acquire it as a possession. We simply become less distracted from what has always been present.

The sun rises from That and returns to That. The powers of life rest in That. Yama keeps widening the circle until there is nowhere left to point and say, “Not there.”

This may be what the refrain is meant to do to us. It slows the mind's habit of dividing reality into sacred and ordinary, spiritual and material, here and there. Again and again, it points to the life before us and says: look more deeply.

Nachiketa came searching for the secret beyond death.

Yama points to the world, to the heart, to the hidden fire, and says:

This, indeed, is That.

**Mantra**

> **This, indeed, is That.**
