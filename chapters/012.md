# Day 12 — Om

> **1.2.15–17**
>
> Death said:
>
> “I will tell you the word toward which the Vedas point, for which people undertake discipline, and for which seekers live lives of simplicity.
>
> “That word is Om.”
>
> This syllable is Brahman. This syllable points to the Highest.
>
> One who understands it finds support in what is beyond passing things.
>
> Om is a sure support, a doorway into the deepest reality.
>
> Knowing its meaning, the seeker comes near to Brahman.

Nachiketa has asked Yama to show him what lies beyond right and wrong, cause and effect, past and future. It is an enormous request. We might expect an equally enormous answer—a complex philosophy, an elaborate description of the cosmos, perhaps a secret teaching requiring many words to explain.

Yama gives him one syllable.

**Om.**

For thousands of years, this sound has held a central place in Indian spiritual practice. Om is not simply a word with a definition that can be substituted for it. It is a sacred sound, spoken and heard, breathed and felt. The Upanishads associate it with Brahman, the ultimate reality underlying all things, as though the mystery that cannot be contained in language might nevertheless be approached through sound.

There is something beautiful about Yama's response. Nachiketa has reached the limits of explanation, and Yama gives him something to practice. Rather than another idea for the mind to grasp, he offers a sound around which the whole person can become still.

We may discover why by simply sounding it slowly. The breath leaves the body. The vibration begins deep within and moves through the chest, throat, and mouth. Then the sound fades into silence. For a moment there is nothing to accomplish and nothing to understand. There is only listening.

Perhaps the silence after Om matters as much as the sound itself.

The mind naturally wants to name, divide, compare, and explain. These are extraordinary capacities, and Yama has never asked Nachiketa to abandon them. But the reality Nachiketa seeks cannot finally become another object placed before the mind. Brahman is not one more thing among other things. It is the mystery within which all things arise.

A sacred sound can interrupt our habit of turning everything into an explanation. We repeat it not because repetition magically produces enlightenment, but because it gathers our scattered attention. The mind wanders into memory or anticipation, and the sound calls us back. Breath by breath, we return.

This is why Yama calls Om a support. We need supports because attention drifts. We become absorbed in what has happened or what might happen, in what we desire or what we fear. A simple word, sound, breath, or bodily sensation can become a place to return when we notice ourselves wandering.

And perhaps returning is itself the practice.

We do not have to force the mind into silence. We do not have to manufacture an encounter with Brahman. We sound Om, listen as the vibration fades, and notice the quiet that was waiting beneath it.

Sound arises.

Sound remains for a moment.

Sound disappears.

Silence remains.

Nachiketa came asking what remains when everything else passes away. Yama has not yet completed his answer.

But perhaps, in the silence after Om, he has begun to let Nachiketa hear it.

**Mantra**

> **Om. I return to the silence beneath all things.**
