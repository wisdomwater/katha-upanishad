# Day 2 — The Courage to Ask

> **1.1.4–6**
>
> Nachiketa said to his father, “Father, to whom will you give me?”
>
> He asked a second time, and then a third.
>
> In anger his father answered, “I give you to Death.”
>
> Nachiketa thought:
>
> Among many I may be first; among many I may be somewhere in the middle. What purpose will Death have for me today?
>
> Look at those who came before us, and look at those who will come after. A human being ripens like grain and falls; like grain, life rises again.

Nachiketa has seen the old cows being given away, and now the discomfort within him becomes a question. If his father is truly giving away everything, then what about his son? “Father, to whom will you give me?” It is the kind of question that exposes the distance between the ritual being performed and the reality it claims to represent.

His father does not answer. So Nachiketa asks again. Then a third time. There is something quietly courageous about his persistence, because he is not merely seeking information. He is pressing against a contradiction that everyone would probably prefer to leave undisturbed.

Eventually his father snaps. “I give you to Death.”

Perhaps the words are spoken in exasperation rather than as a deliberate sentence upon his son. But words spoken in anger can reveal more than we intend, and Nachiketa takes them seriously. Suddenly the question beneath his father's sacrifice becomes much larger. What does it actually mean to give everything? What happens when the thing being surrendered is not an old cow but one's own life?

This is often what a sincere question does. We ask about one thing and discover that the question reaches much farther than we expected. A question about generosity becomes a question about attachment. A question about religious integrity becomes a question about death. A question about someone else's behavior eventually turns toward us.

Nachiketa does not respond to his father's words with outrage. Instead, he considers those who have lived before him and those who will come afterward. Human beings are like grain, he says. We ripen, we fall, and life rises again. He has suddenly placed his own existence inside a much larger rhythm.

There is humility in that recognition. We spend much of life behaving as though death were an interruption to the natural order rather than part of it. We build identities, accumulate possessions, protect reputations, make plans, and quietly assume that the present arrangement should continue. Yet generations rise and fall like fields of grain. The world existed before us, and it will continue after us.

Nachiketa does not yet know what Death will teach him. He has only begun to ask the question that will eventually carry him there. But perhaps wisdom often begins this way: not by possessing the right answers, but by refusing to silence the questions that disturb our comfortable assumptions.

Some questions follow us because they have not yet finished their work.

We do not need to force an answer from them. We may simply need enough courage to keep asking.

**Mantra**

> **I will not turn away from the question that asks to be heard.**
