# Day 6 — Everything You Ever Wanted

> **1.1.23–25**
>
> Death said:
>
> “Choose children and grandchildren who live a hundred years. Choose cattle, elephants, horses, and gold.
>
> “Choose vast lands, and live as many years as you desire.”
>
> “Ask for wealth beyond imagining and a life as long as you wish. Rule a great kingdom.
>
> “I will give you the power to enjoy every desire.”
>
> “Whatever pleasures are difficult for mortals to obtain, ask for them. Take beautiful companions, chariots, music, and every delight. Let them serve you.
>
> “Only do not ask me what lies beyond death.”

Death does not threaten Nachiketa. He tempts him.

The offer is astonishingly generous. Yama places before the boy nearly everything human beings have traditionally desired: family, longevity, wealth, property, power, beauty, music, pleasure, security. Nachiketa can have not merely enough, but abundance. He can live a long life surrounded by everything that makes life desirable.

There is nothing inherently wrong with most of these things. Loving families, material security, music, beauty, and pleasure can all be genuine goods. That is precisely what makes Yama's offer so subtle. Nachiketa is not being asked to choose between obvious good and obvious evil. He is being asked whether good things can distract him from the question he most deeply wants answered.

We know something about this temptation. Most of our lives are filled not with terrible things but with interesting, enjoyable, useful, and desirable things. There is always another experience to have, another possession to acquire, another goal to reach, another problem to solve. Even when we receive what we wanted, another desire quietly appears behind it.

Yama's offer gathers all of this into one extravagant promise: **I will give you the power to enjoy every desire.**

At first this sounds like freedom. Yet there is a hidden problem. If our happiness depends upon satisfying desire, then desire remains our master. We must continually arrange the world so that it gives us what we want, and the world is notoriously unwilling to cooperate. Bodies age. Possessions disappear. relationships change. Pleasures fade. Eventually even the longest life reaches its final day.

The deeper temptation, then, may not be pleasure itself. It is the hope that enough pleasure, security, achievement, or longevity will finally settle the restlessness within us.

We can spend decades testing that hypothesis.

Just a little more money. One more accomplishment. A different body. A little more recognition. Another purchase, meal, relationship, experience, or spiritual insight. There is nothing shameful about wanting these things. But there is wisdom in noticing the promise hidden beneath the wanting: *When I have this, then I will be satisfied.*

Yama offers Nachiketa the ultimate version of that promise.

And then he adds one condition: stop asking about death.

This reveals what is really at stake. The pleasures are not merely gifts; they are distractions from the question Nachiketa has come to ask. Yama is offering him countless ways to fill his life so completely that perhaps he will no longer have to confront its impermanence.

We do not need Yama's chariots or kingdoms to make the same bargain. We can keep ourselves occupied enough that the deeper questions rarely become audible. We can move from desire to desire, allowing each fulfillment to carry us toward the next, until there is little silence left in which to ask what any of it is for.

Nachiketa now stands before everything he could possibly want.

Tomorrow, he will answer.

**Mantra**

> **I notice what I want without asking it to make me whole.**
