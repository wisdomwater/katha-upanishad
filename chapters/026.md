# Day 26 — The Tree with Roots Above

> **2.3.1–4**
>
> There is an ancient tree with its roots above and its branches below.
>
> Its root is the pure, the Brahman, the immortal.
>
> All worlds rest in it, and none passes beyond it.
>
> This, indeed, is That.
>
> The whole universe, whatever exists, arises from and moves within the life of Brahman.
>
> It is a great mystery, like a raised thunderbolt.
>
> Those who truly know it become free from fear.
>
> Through awe before That, fire burns; through awe, the sun shines.
>
> Through awe, the powers of wind and storm move, and Death itself performs its work.
>
> If a person realizes this truth before the body falls away, freedom is found.
>
> If not, one returns again to the worlds of becoming.

Yama now gives Nachiketa another image of the whole of reality: an ancient tree whose roots are above and whose branches grow downward. It reverses what we expect. We normally think of roots as hidden beneath us and branches reaching toward the sky, but Yama turns the image upside down.

The visible world, he suggests, is not the root. Everything we can touch, name, possess, measure, or observe belongs to the branches. The source lies deeper than appearance, in Brahman, the invisible ground from which all things arise.

This image changes the direction of our attention. We naturally become absorbed in the branches: work, relationships, health, possessions, successes, failures, fears, pleasures, and plans. These are the places where daily life happens, and the Upanishad does not dismiss them, but it asks us to remember that they do not sustain themselves.

A branch lives because it remains connected to what nourishes it. Cut off from the root, even the most beautiful branch eventually withers. In the same way, Yama invites Nachiketa to see every finite thing as participating in a reality deeper than itself.

Perhaps some of our fear comes from forgetting the root. We see the branch changing and assume that everything is being lost. Bodies age, circumstances shift, relationships change, and the structures we rely upon prove less permanent than we imagined.

Yama does not deny any of this change. Instead, he asks Nachiketa to look beneath it. If the branches arise from an imperishable root, then change may not be the whole story.

The language of awe that follows can sound severe at first. Fire burns, the sun shines, the wind moves, and even Death performs its work in response to the mystery of Brahman. But this awe need not mean terror before a threatening power; it can mean the recognition that reality is held within an order greater than our individual control.

There is a relief hidden in that recognition. The universe does not depend upon our ability to hold it together. The sun does not wait for our permission to rise, the seasons do not require our management, and life continues through countless processes we neither understand nor direct.

To remember the root is to loosen the illusion that everything rests upon us.

Yama says this realization matters now, before the body falls away. Nachiketa originally came asking what happens after death, but again and again Death brings him back to the present. Freedom is not merely a future destination; it is a way of seeing available within this life.

We can begin by noticing where we have mistaken a branch for the root. Perhaps we have asked achievement to give us identity, security to eliminate uncertainty, another person to make us whole, or a particular future to guarantee peace. These things may be precious, but they cannot bear the weight of being ultimate.

The tree invites us to love the branches without forgetting where their life comes from.

We live among changing forms, yet Yama keeps pointing toward their hidden source. Beneath the movement of the world is the root that does not wither.

This, indeed, is That.

**Mantra**

> **I remember the unseen root beneath the changing branches.**
